# Guiding the Lens

### How Intelligence Specialists Turn a Smear Campaign Against Itself

*by Justin Horn*

---

When someone is trying to make a person look bad, the natural instinct is to hide: stay out of view and give them nothing. Counterintelligence offers another option. Instead of hiding from the adversary's lens, you guide it.

Counterintelligence, at its simplest, means supplying information that the other side collects and believes is true, but isn't. This is the core of what intelligence services call **denial and deception**: denial withholds real information, while deception feeds an adversary false information through a channel they trust. Done well, the target doesn't just fail to learn anything — they become confident they've learned something, and stop looking further. Applied to a smear campaign, it works like this.

**The setup**

Someone has decided to build a case against a person. They're watching closely, looking for anything that can be used as evidence. They already know what they want to find. They just need something to hang it on.

**The move**

The target doesn't hide. Instead, they perform small, subtle actions — a minute or two of miming or gesturing — that would mean nothing to anyone nearby. A casual observer wouldn't notice. Only someone watching intently, hunting for something sinister, would register it at all.

Then they repeat it, daily, for weeks. Consistency does the work.

**Why it works**

People need to make sense of what they see. When an observer already believes something is wrong with the person they're watching, and that person keeps doing the same odd thing on a reliable schedule, a well-documented cognitive bias takes over: **apophenia**, the tendency to perceive meaningful patterns in random or ambiguous information. Once that pattern takes hold, **confirmation bias** locks it in — every later observation gets filtered to fit the story the observer has already started telling themselves. Repeated behavior feels meaningful, and meaningful behavior demands an explanation.

But there's nothing to explain. The behavior is empty, just pretend. So whatever explanation the observer builds has to be fiction. And because the behavior looks deliberate and technical without matching anything real, the fiction they reach for drifts toward the impossible, even science fiction.

The watcher's own intensity drives the result. The closer they look, the less they can actually see. How strongly they end up believing is measured almost exactly by how obsessively they were watching.

**The payoff**

Eventually the adversary puts their conclusion in writing — in a complaint, a statement, or something submitted as evidence. What they've documented isn't misconduct. It's their own invented explanation for nothing. For example, they might claim the person has a device on their car's dashboard that can do things no real technology can do.

Now the adversary has a problem. If they're also telling people the target is unstable, their own written evidence says the target owns a physics-defying device. As a witness, they would have to explain how they reached that conclusion, and there's no answer that doesn't expose their own fixation. The one thing they latched onto to use against the target was supplied by the target.

**The principle**

Every delusion starts as an idea like any other. If an adversary's case rests on their own preconceived idea of who you are, you don't have to fight that idea. You can let them run with it until it becomes something no one else will believe.

It costs nothing. No lies are spoken and no evidence is planted. It takes only a few minutes of playing pretend, done consistently, in front of someone who is watching too closely.
